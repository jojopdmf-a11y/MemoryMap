Plot Trip Stops on a Map from a Spreadsheet (CSV or Excel)

You already know where you’re going. The ports, cities, and overnight stops are sitting in a spreadsheet you built for the cruise, the rail pass, or the road trip. What you want next is simple: those stops on a map you can see, share, or keep — not a tool that reshuffles the order to shave miles off a delivery day.

That gap is why “plot trip stops on a map from a spreadsheet” keeps getting answered with the wrong product. Route optimizers and field-service planners assume you need the fastest driving order. Travelers usually need the opposite: keep the order you already chose (by date or preference) and turn the list into a clear trip map.

You already have the list. You just want the map.

Trip planners live in spreadsheets for a reason. Dates line up in columns. Ports and cities are easy to edit. You can share a sheet with a travel partner without teaching them a new app. The friction starts when you try to see the voyage: typing every stop into Google Maps one pin at a time, or wrestling a multi-step My Maps import when you only wanted a picture of the itinerary.

Route planners solve a different problem. They reorder addresses so a driver can hit more stops with less driving. That is useful for deliveries and service calls. It is not what most vacation lists need. If your sheet is already sequenced by day — embarkation, sea days, port days, return — reordering for shortest path would break the trip.

So the traveler job is narrower and clearer: take the stop list you already trust, drop it onto a map, and walk away with a visual you can keep looking at.

What “plot trip stops from a spreadsheet” should mean

For a vacation itinerary, spreadsheet-to-map should mean:

  1. Drop a CSV or Excel file (or paste a viewable Google Sheets link, or type the stops).
  2. First row = headers.
  3. Required shape: a date and a place — or city, state, and country.
  4. Optional: title and notes. Latitude and longitude are optional too; place names can be looked up.
  5. Numbers users: export to Excel or CSV first. A native Numbers file is not a drop-in format on MemoryMap’s public preview.

That is trip-shaped data. It is not the same as a delivery CSV full of street addresses, customer IDs, and time windows. City-style stops are enough for cruise ports, rail hubs, and road-trip overnight towns. Street-level coordinates help when you have them, but they should not be required to get a useful map.

If your sheet only puts place names in a free-text Notes column and leaves Place / City blank, most importers will not know what to plot. Put the place where a header can find it.

What you get after plotting

After you plot, you should be able to:

  • See stops on a map in itinerary order (not a re-optimized delivery route).
  • Review the list next to the map.
  • Download a souvenir map file when the preview offers it.

That is the traveler outcome: list in, map out, keep a copy if you want one.

Spreadsheet tips that prevent empty maps

  • Put date and place (or city/state/country) in their own columns with clear headers.
  • Keep the trip order you want — by date is usually enough.
  • Prefer city/port names over a paragraph of notes when you only need overview pins.
  • Export Numbers to CSV/Excel before dropping.
  • If a place fails to resolve, tighten the place text (add country) or add lat/lon when you have them.

When a route optimizer is the wrong tool

Use a route optimizer when the job is “hit these addresses with less driving.” Use a trip map when the job is “show the voyage I already planned.” Mixing those jobs is why spreadsheet-to-map searches so often feel like the wrong answer.
